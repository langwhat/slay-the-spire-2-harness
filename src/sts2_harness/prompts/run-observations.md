# Run basics

These describe ordinary play. Apply native rules and the currently offered choices when they alter that flow.

- A run progresses through connected rooms and acts. Follow public map connections and currently reachable choices. Room types can include battles, elites, shops, rest sites, treasure, events, and Bosses. Unrevealed room contents and outcomes remain unknown.
- HP, max HP, gold, the permanent deck, relics, and remaining potions matter across rooms. Do not assume battle completion restores HP or other resources; use the returned state and the effects that actually apply.
- Adding, removing, transforming, and upgrading permanent deck cards can affect later battles. Battle piles are not the permanent deck: discarding or exhausting a card in battle is not permanent removal. Temporary and permanent changes are distinguished by native text and the observed deck.
- In battle, cards use their shown resources and affect HP, block, enemies, powers, or other cards. Cards are drawn into the hand, normally move to discard after play or at the turn transition, and may instead be retained, exhausted, or moved by effects. A draw first takes the remaining draw-pile cards; if further cards are needed after the pile empties, discard is shuffled to form a new draw pile and drawing continues if possible. This circulation matters when judging deck changes and likely access to cards.
- Resources, draw amounts, block retention, and power duration depend on game rules and effects. Consider native card text, relics, and powers together; do not assess a purchase or upgrade only by its printed base number.
- Rewards offer the choices shown. Take or decline only where that option exists. A full potion inventory can require a supported replacement or discard choice; do not assume capacity or automatic replacement.
- Shop prices and services are current facts. Purchases can spend resources, change later offers, or open a selection. Rest sites and events expose their actual available operations; some results are unknown until chosen.
- Potions have limited slots and are consumed on use. Use them outside battle only when the current state permits it.

# Planning and notes

Judge choices by how they change the run's ability to survive and win upcoming fights. Skipping a reward or buying nothing is the baseline each addition must beat. Consider all offered alternatives, including declining a reward, buying nothing, and smaller purchase sets. Do not add a card merely because it is useful in isolation, shares a theme, or can receive an existing bonus.

Maintain a concrete account of the deck's capabilities: damage and target coverage, defense, growth, access to important cards, and the time and resources needed to establish them. Distinguish dependable capabilities from occasional combinations and incomplete plans. Use the latest battle review and notes as evidence, then check their conclusions against the cards, relics, current resources, and coming threats. A review's suggested card category is not a restriction on the available solutions.

For each serious candidate, compare a plausible turn or sequence with and without it. Identify what it adds, when that addition matters, what it costs to draw and play, and what useful play it displaces. Adding a card does not remove a weaker one: when drawn, it takes the place of whatever the current deck would otherwise have supplied, which may be a key card or a stronger card in the same role. Compare it with that displaced draw and with the deck's existing providers of the needed effect, not merely with the weakest card it outperforms. A partial improvement can be worthwhile without solving every problem, but identify its actual contribution. Do not conclude that a card fixes defense merely because it gives block, or improves access merely because it draws cards. Compare the output and timing with the size and duration of the demonstrated need.

Evaluate effects using the resources the deck can actually establish. Separate a setup cost paid once from costs paid on every later use. If existing growth also increases another type of output, evaluate that conversion explicitly rather than judging the card only at zero growth. Compare the whole playable turn: a costly card may leave fewer actions while providing much more of the needed effect; several cheap cards may require more draws or a difficult sequence. Include realistic draws before setup and after setup, not only the strongest combination or the opening hand. Do not assume that an undemonstrated resource engine will work, or that one not used in the last battle cannot work.

For conditional effects, check the required events, order, same-turn access, and remaining energy. Distinguish reusable support from a card that exhausts and helps only the first cycle. Count mutually exclusive uses separately. Draw, filtering, retrieval, and exhaustion change access and the later draw pool, but they have their own costs and constraints. A larger deck can dilute weak starter cards or delay crucial cards; compare the resulting draw quality rather than deck size alone.

When considering a duplicate, identify the existing cards and relics that share its effect. Determine whether their effects stack, share a counter or decay clock, improve access to a scarce payoff, or merely repeat an already dependable role. Compare the added copy's contribution over the relevant turns. Also compare it with reaching the existing copies more often through draw, energy, or removal, and with waiting for a stronger card from later rewards, which an adequate addition would also dilute. An existing damage bonus is not by itself a reason to add every compatible attack; an existing defensive effect is not by itself a reason to reject another source that compounds it. When a duplicate or interaction drives the decision, name that interaction in the short action reason.

Early in the run, favor useful immediate output or a direction attainable with modest support across several threats. As the deck gains a dependable core, raise the burden on additional general-purpose cards: check whether they improve a remaining weakness or compete with stronger existing plays. Continue to consider unusually valuable investments and upgrades. Do not assume a missing component will appear, but do not reject a powerful conversion of resources already present merely because it is costly or needs setup.

Rarity and upgrade status can explain real differences in output, flexibility, or opportunity cost; neither is an automatic ranking. Evaluate the offered version. If an upgrade changes the decision, compare reachable upgrade opportunities and the existing cards competing for them; an added card that matters mainly once upgraded takes an upgrade from those cards. Include the possible need to rest. Do not price a hypothetical upgraded card as though it were already upgraded. Usually prioritize removing weak starter attack and block cards, while checking actual removal eligibility, synergies, and opportunity costs.

At shops, a card needs to justify both its permanent deck slot and the gold spent on it. Compare its incremental capability with useful affordable relics, removal, potions, combinations, and keeping the gold. Do not count a possible combination as a new capability if the deck already reliably supplies the needed outcome. Use actual prices and acquisition triggers; buying an extra item just to advance a counter can still be worse. Keep the whole purchase plan affordable and re-evaluate after each reveal or change. Saving gold is useful only to the extent that remaining opportunities can use it; spending all available gold is not a goal.

Compare upgrades with healing by their repeated effect across the reachable route; HP is a resource for winning fights and reaching valuable routes, not a goal in itself. When a reachable rest site still comes before the main threat, healing now and upgrading later keep that site just as upgrading now does; the plans differ in HP and card strength during the fights in between, healing lost to the maximum-HP cap, and whether a second upgrade remains possible. Heal now for a concrete intervening danger: a reachable fight whose plausible poor draws current HP and potions cannot cover, or a more valuable resource that healing preserves. When you rest in that situation, name that danger in the short reason. Count a rest site as healing or upgrading unless its actual rules allow both. At forks, compare the threats, rewards, recovery and fallback routes still reachable after a bad fight. Account for remaining special moves. When rewards are similar, value flexibility without automatically choosing the safest route. Reassess after substantial gains, losses or spending; do not use fixed HP thresholds or elite counts.

Treat separate reward groups as separate choices. Assess the combination left after earlier picks, and resolve each group rather than assuming that choosing one card settles every offer. Earlier actions can change later resources, options, references and legality. Combine supported actions according to run_act's execution and recovery rules.
